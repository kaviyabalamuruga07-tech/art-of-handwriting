Copperplate

Formal Cursive · 1600s

Born when engravers translated calligraphic letterforms onto copper printing plates, Copperplate is defined by dramatic thick-to-thin contrast executed with a flexible pointed nib. Heavy pressure on downstrokes, near-weightless upstrokes — the gold standard of formal correspondence for three centuries.

Blackletter

Gothic Script · 1100s

Blackletter dominated European manuscripts from the 12th century through the Renaissance. Its dense, compressed letterforms emerged from scribes' need to fit more text onto expensive vellum. Sub-styles include Textura (used in the Gutenberg Bible), Rotunda, and Fraktur — Germany's national hand until the 20th century.

Broad-edge nibDense textureAngular formsDiamond serifs

Humanist Italic

Renaissance Script · 1400s

Italic was born in the courts of Renaissance Italy as humanist scholars sought to revive Roman clarity. Slanted, compressed, and flowing yet highly legible, it was adopted by the Papal Chancery and cast in metal by Aldus Manutius in 1501 — making it the first typeface modelled directly on a handwriting style.

5–10° slantBranching joinsCompressed widthHumanist forms

Spencerian

American Script · 1850s

Platt Rogers Spencer's graceful system balanced flowing oval forms with delicate hairline upstrokes — lighter than Copperplate yet refined. It became the dominant business hand in 19th-century America. The Coca-Cola logo has been penned in Spencerian script since 1887.

Casual Script

Everyday · Modern

Casual handwriting is the living language of everyday life — grocery lists, journals, birthday cards. Defined by personality rather than rules, it evolved as mass education democratized literacy and the ballpoint pen replaced the fountain pen. Graphologists call it the most intimate script: every stroke a subconscious self-portrait.

Brush Lettering

Modern Calligraphy · 2010s

Drawing from East Asian calligraphic traditions — where the brush has been the premier instrument for millennia — and from 20th-century sign painting, brush lettering has enjoyed an extraordinary contemporary revival. Its visible human imperfection is valued precisely in a world of perfect pixel typography.

Retro Script

Mid-Century · 1950s

Rooted in American mid-century commercial lettering — surf culture, diners, drive-ins — retro script combines the friendliness of a brush letter with the boldness needed for signage. Rounded, bouncy, optimistic. Its visual vocabulary was shaped by hand-painted storefronts and the golden age of advertising.

Condensed Print

Architectural · Contemporary

Condensed hand-printing draws from architectural lettering traditions — the tall, narrow all-caps script used by draughtsmen, architects, and engineers before CAD replaced pencil. Prized for its clean legibility at small sizes and used extensively in editorial design, signage, and hand-lettered posters.

Rough Chalk

Vernacular · Chalk

Derived from the thick, inconsistent strokes of chalk on a blackboard or stub pencil on rough paper, this style celebrates texture and imperfection. Popularised in contemporary café chalkboard design and artisan branding, it carries an authenticity that smooth digital type cannot replicate.

Marker Script

Street · Protest

The handwriting of urgency — democratised by the felt-tip marker in 1910 and risen to prominence in protest signs, street art, and graffiti. Uncompromising, bold, entirely legible from a distance. Its visual authority comes from bluntness: no room for flourish, only message.

Evoking the warm idiosyncrasies of a grandparent's handwriting — slightly wobbly, entirely individual, warmly imperfect. This style captures the aesthetic of personal letters, recipe cards, and family documents from the mid-20th century. Its charm is inseparable from its imprecision.

Pinyon Script

Victorian Revival · 1800s

A refined pointed-pen script in the Victorian tradition, Pinyon balances the formal elegance of Copperplate with a slightly lighter, more open character. Its letterforms feel at home on invitations, certificates, and menus — evoking gaslit parlours and the era when correspondence was a considered art form.

Inspired by informal 1950s–1970s handwriting, it carries joyful approachable energy suited to greeting cards, children's books, and any communication that wants to feel genuinely warm.

Italianno

Italian Fine Script

Drawing from the Italian cursive tradition, Italianno is an elongated, freely drawn script with gentle pressure variation and elegant connecting strokes. Where Copperplate is architectural, Italianno is lyrical — letters feel drawn rather than constructed, with a calligraphic spontaneity that rewards a relaxed hand.

Niconne

Airy Script · Modern

Niconne evokes letters written quickly with a light hand — a diary entry, a note left on a kitchen table, a postcard signature. Its forms are casual yet composed, with a natural forward lean and open loops that give text an airy, unhurried quality. It sits midway between calligraphic script and natural handwriting.

Romantic Script

Victorian Romantic

Romantic scripts carry the emotional charge of letters written in candlelight — impassioned, slightly reckless, undeniably beautiful. Their letterforms prioritise feeling over precision, with dramatic flourishes and ink pooling at stroke endings that suggest a quill pressed hard into the page during a moment of sincere feeling.

Ruthie

Fine Flourished Script

An exquisitely light fine script — all hairlines and the thinnest of flex strokes, resembling the writing of a highly trained Victorian penman at their most restrained. The letters are widely spaced with swelling terminals, producing text that appears almost suspended in air, impossibly delicate on the page.

Caveat captures the unguarded quality of handwriting in a personal notebook — neither practised calligraphy nor careless scrawl, but the everyday cursive of a thoughtful person writing quickly for themselves. Its slight inconsistency and naturalistic letterforms make it the digital era's most honest script.
